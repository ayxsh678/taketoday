The fintech playbook of the 2010s was explicit: identify a financial service that requires regulatory approval to provide, find the adjacent service that doesn't, build there, and grow until you're too large to ignore. The strategy worked. It produced Robinhood, Klarna, Chime, Revolut, and dozens of others worth tens of billions of dollars.

That window is closing. Simultaneously, in every major market, regulators have concluded that the policy rationale for treating fintech differently than finance no longer holds.

### The BNPL reckoning

Buy-now-pay-later is the clearest case. In 2020, BNPL companies argued they offered a payment product, not a credit product, and therefore shouldn't be subject to consumer credit regulations. Regulators and the industry reached an informal arrangement: move carefully, don't cause obvious consumer harm, and we'll figure out the rules together.

That arrangement has ended. The CFPB's new rules treat BNPL explicitly as consumer credit. The FCA in the UK finalized similar language in 2025. Australia brought BNPL under the National Consumer Credit Protection Act.

The implications are concrete: BNPL companies now face credit reporting requirements, dispute resolution obligations, affordability assessment mandates, and capital adequacy rules they previously avoided. Klarna's IPO prospectus acknowledges this directly. Affirm's 2025 10-K devotes 12 pages to regulatory risk. These are not boilerplate disclosures.

The margin math on BNPL depended on avoiding those costs. With them, the business model at the thin-ticket end — the $50 fashion purchase split into four payments — is structurally unprofitable at current merchant discount rates.

### The winners of the compliance tightening

The counterintuitive beneficiaries are the fintechs that over-invested in compliance when they didn't have to. Stripe's years of investment in financial crime compliance infrastructure — before regulators required it — now operates as a selling point in enterprise contract negotiations. Marqeta's banking partnerships required compliance parity with bank standards years before the regulatory landscape made that mandatory for competitors.

> "We spent $40M on compliance between 2019 and 2022 that we thought was wasted money. It turns out we were pre-paying for a competitive moat." — CFO of a Series D payments company, anonymized interview, Q1 2026.

*Quotes attributed to roles rather than named individuals are anonymized from direct interviews; identifying details have been removed.*

The companies that built compliance as a feature, not a cost center, are now selling access to that infrastructure to fintechs that didn't. Compliance-as-a-service is a real and growing business.

### The consolidation effect

The third outcome — and perhaps the most transformative — is what happens to the fintechs that can't absorb the compliance cost spike. They're selling.

Bank M&A of fintech assets hit a record in 2025, not because banks suddenly valued innovation, but because banks see a path to acquiring technology at distressed valuations while the fintech's regulatory liabilities are manageable before growth compounds them. JPMorgan, BBVA, and DBS have been the most active acquirers.

The fintech era isn't ending. But its structure is being reset around compliance as a floor, not an optional feature. The companies that treated compliance as optional are learning that it was actually the product.
