The first enforcement deadline under the EU AI Act came and went last week. The regulators didn't make a scene.

Pre-deadline, industry lobbying warned of a wave of enforcement actions, product withdrawals, and innovation flight. The reality so far: no significant fines, no headline-grabbing takedowns, and a notably quiet press posture from the Commission.

That does not mean the Act isn't working. It means it's working the way regulations actually work — in documents, not press releases.

### What companies are actually doing

Inside legal teams at large model providers, the work has been intense but internal. Risk classifications for every deployed use case. Data-provenance documentation for training corpora. Bias-testing reports signed off by named individuals. Internal "AI bill of materials" — essentially SBOMs for models — tracking every component and dataset touched.

None of this is visible externally. All of it takes months of engineering and legal time.

### The part nobody is talking about

Smaller AI vendors — particularly US startups with EU users but no EU entity — are taking a different path. Geo-blocking. Several have quietly disabled EU access rather than stand up the compliance infrastructure. The Act's ambition was to shape global AI development. Its actual early effect is to shrink the EU's share of AI products available to consumers.

Whether that trade is worth it is a policy question the Commission has not publicly answered.

### The "documentation tax" framing

One head of legal we spoke with, at a top-five model provider, called the Act "the documentation tax." The description captures something important. The regulation is less about stopping specific behaviors and more about forcing firms to write down, in defensible detail, what they're doing and why. That creates a paper trail a regulator can act on later, at will.

It's a slow-release mechanism. The enforcement didn't happen in April. The enforcement capacity has been built, and is now loaded.

### What to watch

The first actual fine. When it lands, and against whom, will tell us what the Commission actually cares about. A large American firm gets made an example of. A European national champion gets a pass. The symmetry — or lack of it — will define the next two years of compliance strategy across the industry.
