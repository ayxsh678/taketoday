In 2021, Indian startups raised $42B. In 2025, they raised $16B. That's not a correction. That's a different market.

The narrative around Indian startup funding tends to frame this as a cyclical problem — global rates went up, risk appetite went down, the cycle will turn. That reading misses what actually changed structurally in the Indian ecosystem, and why the companies emerging from this period are fundamentally different from the 2021 cohort.

### The 2021 vintage problem

The 2021 funding surge was driven by a specific set of conditions: near-zero global rates, COVID-era digital acceleration, and a glut of Tiger Global-style tourists who didn't underwrite Indian market fundamentals. They underwrote growth rates.

The companies that raised in that environment built for growth. Customer acquisition costs didn't matter if the round was coming. Burn multiples were academic. The question was always: what does the next round look like?

The answer in 2024 and 2025 was: it looks like a down round, or it doesn't come. The Indian consumer internet space — edtech, quick commerce, social commerce — saw more write-downs, shutdowns, and distressed sales in 2024 than the preceding four years combined.

> "We funded 60 companies in 2021. I'm not proud of all of them. The discipline that the market has imposed in the last two years is painful but correct." — Partner at a top-tier India-focused VC, anonymized interview, February 2026.

### What the reset looks like on the ground

Byju's became the poster child, but the structural story is broader. Quick commerce consolidation is real: Zepto, Blinkit, and Swiggy Instamart are fighting for a market that was supposed to have six players by now. The three that remain are actually profitable on an EBITDA basis in major cities — something none of them were in 2021.

Fintech has bifurcated. Regulated, profitable fintechs with banking licenses or NBFC status are raising successfully, often at flat or modest valuations. Unregulated credit plays built on loopholes have been shut down or are quietly pivoting.

Edtech is the sector that absorbed the most capital and produced the least durable value. The survivors are niche, vocational, and B2B. The B2C mass-market play — Byju's, Unacademy, Vedantu — is structurally broken by the return of offline education and the inability to monetize at Indian price points.

### The AI exception

The one segment attracting 2021-style enthusiasm is AI infrastructure and enterprise software. Indian founders building AI-native products for global markets — not attempting to replicate consumer apps for domestic users — are closing rounds at multiples that look out of place in the broader landscape.

This isn't irrational. Indian engineering talent is globally competitive, and building AI infrastructure doesn't require the distribution advantages that domestic consumer plays need. The market is the world, the cost structure is Indian, and the investors writing checks are the same ones who've rotated out of consumer internet.

The reset has been brutal for the 2021 cohort. For the 2024–2026 cohort, it's a cleaner slate.
